The 14 bus has 61 stops along its seven miles. That's one about every 600 feet, roughly two short city blocks. Riders like having a stop close by, and nobody wants theirs to be the one that goes. But every stop costs time, and on the 14 those stops add up to about a quarter of the trip.

## The arithmetic of a stop

Each time a bus stops, it slows down, opens the doors, waits, closes them and pulls back into traffic. Even when only one person gets on, that's around twenty to thirty seconds. Across 61 stops, the 14 spends more time stopping than it does in traffic.

Many cities space stops about a quarter mile apart, roughly 1,300 feet. Using that spacing on the 14 would mean about 30 stops instead of 61, and the authority's own model estimates the trip would get six to eight minutes faster. For most riders, the walk to the nearest stop would grow by about a minute.

## Who pays for the faster trip

That minute isn't evenly shared. For someone with a cane, a walker or heavy bags, an extra block matters far more than it does for me. This is where stop consolidation goes wrong in a lot of cities: a planner removes stops by spacing alone, and the stop outside the senior center goes.

The better practice is to start with a short list of stops that stay no matter what: hospitals, clinics, senior housing, schools, big transfer points. Space the rest around them. Then walk the route with people who use it before anything is final.

## What would help

The authority plans to study the 14 next year. If you ride it, ask to be included in the walk-through, and suggest the stops that should never move. A faster bus that leaves people behind isn't faster for them.
