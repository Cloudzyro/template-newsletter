Everyone has a theory about crosswalk buttons. They're placebos. They only work at night. You have to press them three times. After a reader asked, I spent a week with a traffic engineer from the city who agreed to talk as long as I didn't use her name, and the truth is more useful than any of the theories.

## Three kinds of button

There are roughly three kinds of button in Corliss.

The first kind does real work. At intersections where people rarely cross, the walk signal only appears if someone asks for it. Press it, and the next cycle includes a walk phase. Don't press it, and you'll wait through a green light with a red hand.

The second kind is on a fixed schedule. Downtown, every cycle includes a walk phase whether you press or not. The button still exists, often because it also triggers the audible signal for blind pedestrians, which matters a great deal to the people who use it.

The third kind is the one that frustrates people: buttons that register your press but only act on it at a set point in the cycle. Press just after that point and you wait a full cycle, up to two minutes, with no sign that anything happened.

## How to tell

Look for a small light or a sound when you press. Since 2021, new buttons in Corliss confirm a press, but about four hundred older ones don't. If the walk signal comes up at the same moment the parallel traffic turns green, every time, you're probably at a fixed-schedule crossing.

## What would help

The engineer's wish is simple: confirmation lights on every button, so people stop guessing and stop crossing against the signal out of frustration. The city has a list of the four hundred older ones. At about $300 each, replacing them would cost less than one block of repaving. It's a fair thing to ask about at the next budget hearing.
