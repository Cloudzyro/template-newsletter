Stand at a curb ramp for an hour and count who uses it. I did, at Ferry Road and 3rd, on a Tuesday afternoon. Two wheelchair users. Fourteen strollers. Nine people with rolling suitcases or shopping carts. Six cyclists walking their bikes. One man moving a refrigerator on a dolly. Everyone, it turns out, likes not having to lift something over a curb.

## The backlog

Federal rules require curb ramps at crossings when a street is resurfaced. In practice, that means corners get ramps only when their street comes up for repaving, on a cycle that can run twenty years. Corliss's public works department estimates that 2,100 corners still have no ramp at all, and another 3,400 have ramps that no longer meet the standard because they're too steep or have no textured warning strip.

At the current pace of about 150 corners a year, the backlog clears sometime in the 2050s.

## Why it's slow

A single ramp costs between $4,000 and $12,000, depending on drainage and whether a utility box is in the way. Most of the cost is design and coordination, not concrete. Several cities have sped up by bundling: one contract covers a hundred corners in one neighborhood, designed together, with one crew working through them in a season.

## What would help

Corliss has a transition plan for accessibility, as it's required to. It's posted on the public works site and it hasn't been updated since 2017. Asking for an updated plan with a bundled schedule, and a public map of which corners are next, would make the backlog visible. Things that are visible tend to get funded.

If you know a corner that's especially bad, the city's service request line accepts ramp requests. They go on a list, and corners with requests move up it.
