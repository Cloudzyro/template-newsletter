Pull up the Corliss transit map and you'll see 41 colored lines, all the same thickness. The 9, which comes every forty minutes on Saturdays, looks exactly like the 22, which comes every twelve minutes all day. For a rider trying to plan a trip, that's the most important fact about a route, and the map hides it.

## Frequent network maps

Some cities draw what's called a frequent network map. Routes that come at least every fifteen minutes, all day, get a thick line in a strong color. Everything else is thin and grey. The difference is immediate: you can see at a glance which streets you can just walk out to and catch a bus, and which ones need a timetable.

When I sketched one for Corliss, something stood out. Only five routes qualify, and they form two lines crossing downtown and not much else. The map makes the network's shape obvious in a way the official one never does.

## Why it matters beyond riders

A frequent network map changes conversations about housing and jobs too. Developers, employers and planners all use transit maps to decide where to build. If the map shows where service is actually good, new homes and offices tend to cluster there, which brings more riders, which justifies more service. It's one of the cheapest planning tools a city has.

## What would help

Making the map costs almost nothing. The authority already has the schedule data, and a designer could draw it in a week. I've asked whether the next printed map could include a frequent network version, and the answer was a polite maybe. A few more people asking might turn that into a yes. The authority's customer feedback form is the right place, and it does get read.
