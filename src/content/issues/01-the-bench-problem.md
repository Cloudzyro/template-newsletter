Last spring the bench at the Holloway Avenue stop vanished. No notice, no sign, just four bolt holes in the concrete and a few riders standing where they used to sit. I asked around, and the answer turned out to be more interesting than vandalism.

## Who owns a bench

In Corliss, the transit authority owns the bus stop sign. The city owns the sidewalk. And the bench, in most cases, belongs to an advertising company that installs it for free in exchange for the panel on its back. When ad sales on a corridor drop below a set level, the contract lets the company pull the bench. Holloway's ad revenue fell after the hardware store closed, and the bench went with it.

That arrangement made sense in 1998, when the contract was signed. It meant hundreds of benches at no cost to the city. It also meant that where you could sit depended on what advertisers thought of your street, which is a strange way to decide where people wait for a bus.

## The forty-dollar rule

The city could buy its own benches. A plain steel one costs about $1,100 installed. The catch is maintenance: under the current budget rules, anything the city installs on a sidewalk needs a line item for upkeep, estimated at forty dollars a year. Forty dollars sounds like nothing, but no department has a line for it, so no department buys benches.

Other cities have solved this in boring ways. Some put bench upkeep inside the transit budget. Some let neighborhood groups adopt a stop. A few simply decided that seating at stops with more than fifty daily boardings is part of the service, like the sign.

## What would help

The ad contract comes up for renewal next June. That's the moment to ask for a clause that keeps benches at busy stops regardless of ad sales. If you ride a bus in Corliss, the transit board's public comment period opens in March, and a two-sentence email is enough to get it on the record.
