title: Night buses and who rides them

Corliss runs three bus routes after midnight: the N1, N4 and N9. Together they carry about 900 people a night. When the transit authority looked for savings two years ago, the night routes were first on the list, because on paper they're the most expensive rides in the system.

## Cost per rider

That paper measure is cost per boarding: what it costs to run a route divided by how many people get on. Night buses look bad on it, around $11 per boarding against $4 for the average daytime route. Fewer riders, same driver.

But the measure hides who those riders are. The authority's last onboard survey found that 68 percent of night riders were going to or from work, against 41 percent in the daytime. Hospital staff, bakery shifts, office cleaners, airport workers. For most of them, there is no other way to get there, and losing the bus means losing the job.

## What a cut would have saved

The proposal would have saved about $1.9 million a year, a little under one percent of the operating budget. It was withdrawn after two hospitals and the largest bakery in the city wrote letters, and the board asked for a different measure: how many riders have no other option.

That measure exists. Some agencies call it transit dependence, and they weigh it alongside cost when deciding what to cut. Corliss doesn't use it yet. Writing it into the service standards would mean the next round of cuts starts from who needs a route, not just what it costs.

## What would help

The service standards are reviewed every three years, and the next review starts in February. It's a dry document, but it's the rulebook every future cut follows. If you or someone you know rides at night, a short note to the board describing the trip is worth more than you'd think.
